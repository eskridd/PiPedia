# About Pi

**π** (usually written *pi* in English) is the number you get when you divide a circle's circumference by its diameter. It comes out the same for every circle, which is a big part of why mathematicians love it.

$$\pi \approx 3.14159\ 26535\ 89793\ 23846\ 26433\ 83279\ 50288$$

## Definition

For any circle, big or small:

$$\pi = \frac{C}{d} = \frac{\text{circumference}}{\text{diameter}} \approx 3.14159$$

Two things make π special. It is **irrational**, so it can't be written as a fraction of two whole numbers. It is also **transcendental**, so it isn't the solution of any polynomial with rational coefficients. Its digits never repeat and never settle into a pattern.

## Classic formulas involving π

| Formula | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $C = 2\pi r$ | Circumference of a circle |
| $A = \pi r^2$ | Area of a circle |
| $V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ | Volume of a sphere |
| $\sin(x)$ periodic with $2\pi$ | Waves and oscillations |
| $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$ | Euler's identity |

## Infinite series

The Leibniz formula is lovely to look at and painfully slow to use:

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + \cdots$$

Euler cracked the Basel problem in 1734:

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$$

Ramanujan-style series converge absurdly fast, and they power the record calculations of today.

## A brief history

- **~1900 BC:** Babylonian and Egyptian scribes estimate π at about 3.125
- **~250 BC:** Archimedes squeezes π between $\tfrac{223}{71}$ and $\tfrac{22}{7}$ using polygons
- **~480 AD:** Zu Chongzhi lands on $\pi \approx \tfrac{355}{113}$, good to six decimals
- **1706:** William Jones picks the letter π for the constant; Euler later makes it famous
- **1949:** The ENIAC computer grinds out 2,037 digits
- **2020s:** The record now sits past 100 trillion digits
